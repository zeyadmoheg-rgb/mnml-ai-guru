# Unified Creative Direction — Mahmoud El Behiry × Hussein Mardini

Research-based style dossiers and a meet-in-the-middle creative direction for a
shared project, prepared for the creative director's treatment. Sources: both
artists' portfolio sites (fetched directly, including sample frames analyzed
visually), Mardini's press record (CairoScene, WePresent, Nowness), and web
research. Instagram was login-walled for both; nothing below is inferred from it.

---

## ARTIST 1 — MAHMOUD EL BEHIRY

### Positioning & background
Born 1997. Fashion/commercial photographer working between **Cairo and Dubai**,
self-described as having "a refined aesthetic and a deep passion for fashion
photography," "known for working with top celebrities and luxury fashion
brands." Career stage: **established regional commercial photographer on the
Egypt–Gulf luxury circuit** — a working professional whose brand is his client
list, not a public voice. Notably, **no interviews, profiles, or press features
were found**; his entire public identity is the portfolio itself.

### Signature visual language (from direct image analysis)
- **Light:** Two registers. On location: hard, low, directional sunlight used
  graphically — shadow patterns cast across walls, the model placed at the seam
  of light and shade (Vogue editorial in an old Dubai souk: model in cream
  against an oxide-red door, lattice shadows raking the wall). In studio:
  controlled, flattering beauty light on seamless backgrounds (Okhtein on grey
  seamless; KOJAK against pink theatrical drapery).
- **Color:** Disciplined, warm mineral palettes — terracotta, cream, ochre,
  dust — with deliberate single-accent color stories (a saturated yellow look
  against whitewashed Nubian architecture for Marie Claire; monochrome black on
  grey for Okhtein; pink-on-silver camp for KOJAK).
- **Composition:** Centered, frontal, full-length figure against architecture
  or drapery. Symmetry, doorframes, verticals. The garment is always fully
  legible — silhouette first.
- **Texture/finish:** Clean, high-resolution, flawlessly retouched. Volumized,
  art-directed hair; polished skin; no grain, no motion blur, no accident.

### Subject treatment
Models are rendered as **poised, sculptural mannequin-muses** — elongated,
composed, emotionally cool, gaze detached. Even in "real" places (souk, Nubian
village) the location is treated as **set dressing**: sanitized, emptied of
locals, arranged around the garment. Emotion is aesthetic, not narrative.
People do not have interiority in his frames; they have posture.

### Tone & sensibility
Polished, controlled, pre-visualized, client-safe. Craft-forward rather than
concept-forward. There is genuine sophistication in his light (the souk shadow
work is not generic e-comm — it's graphic and intentional), but the sensibility
is **service aesthetics**: it flatters the brand, the celebrity, the garment.

### Clients / publications & tier signal
Vogue and Marie Claire editorials (almost certainly regional/Arabia editions —
**unverified which**, flag below), PAP Magazine; brands Okhtein, KOJAK, Hechter
Paris, Kooheji Jewellery, LA BLANCA, Maison Saedi, Asteri Beauty, LINN; Gulf
celebrities Fahad AlKubaisi, Assala, Shahira Lasheen. Signal: **top of the
regional commercial market, not (yet) an international editorial name.**

### Values / avoids
- **Values:** control, refinement, garment fidelity, color discipline,
  repeatable professional finish, client satisfaction.
- **Avoids:** mess, spontaneity, unstyled reality, grain, political or
  subcultural content, anything that risks the deliverable.

### Adjectives
**Polished · poised · graphic · sun-carved · luxurious · controlled**

## ARTIST 2 — HUSSEIN MARDINI

### Positioning & background
23-year-old director/cinematographer/photographer, self-identified "Levantine
Cairene" (Turkish-Syrian roots). Studied economics in London, returned to Cairo
in 2025 ("Why am I still paying rent in London? Let me move back to Cairo…
that was the best decision I have made"). Founded his own production house,
**Unscene**, "because of how fucked up the industry really is." Career stage:
**fast-rising auteur** — real press narrative (CairoScene profile, WePresent
commission, Nowness "In Motion" selection), scaling quickly (claims a recent
commercial employed 200 people). Aspires openly to a Scorsese-scale authorial
career centered on Cairene identity.

### Signature visual language (from films, press, and direct image analysis)
- **Light:** Available and natural light bias — dusk, low light, window light.
  Nubia series: grainy, muted dusk on the Nile. Tul8te GQ MENA cover: moody
  interior daylight through curtains, desaturated green-teal domestic gloom.
  For dance work (Ballet du Caire): **high-contrast, slow-motion** treatment.
- **Color:** Muted, filmic, desaturated palettes with visible **grain and
  texture**; warm dust tones and Nile blue-greys; color from the world, not
  from gels.
- **Composition:** Two modes — observational documentary frames, and
  **composed frontal tableaux packed with real life** (Saint Levant series: a
  brass band draped over and through a white Cairo microbus in an alley —
  staged symmetry, genuinely inhabited).
- **Camera/kit:** Light and mobile — Sony FX3 with a 28-70mm f/2 (credited on
  "A Portrait of Cairo"). Run-and-gun capable; embeds in crowds.
- **Sound as design:** cut the moulid film with no added music — "the praise
  and chants playing throughout the moulid were all a part of this experience."

### Subject treatment
People are **protagonists with interiority**: men weeping in trance at moulids,
strangers embracing, kids in Nubia, street-cast ensembles. Place is a
**character, not a backdrop** — "This film isn't an attempt to explain this
city, but it is only a mere reflection of how I experience Cairo." Explicitly
anti-orientalist: refuses to romanticize or exoticize; frames shaabi dance as
fine art ("This is our ballet"). Garments and celebrity (Saint Levant, Wegz,
Tul8te, Hend Sabry) are folded into cultural narrative rather than isolated.

### Tone & sensibility
Raw, instinctive, politically charged, self-consciously authorial. Directs by
feel, not technical language ("This is what I hear, this is what I see").
Openly angry — at Western perceptions of Egypt, at class bias, at the
algorithm — and channels it: "I am a storyteller not an influencer." Also
openly domineering on set, by his own words: **"I'm like a toxic boyfriend
with my ideas. Anybody who comes near, I'm like, 'Shut the fuck up, please.'"**

### Clients / publications & tier signal
Nowness, WePresent (WeTransfer), GQ MENA (two covers), Vogue (Saint Levant
editorial), Rolling Stone, Dazed, Billboard Arabia, Gucci ("La Famiglia"),
Kotn (multiple campaigns incl. World Cup spot with Wegz), Sony, Atmos,
Wonderful Indonesia, Experience AlUla. Signal: **international culture-title
validation + brand work that hires him for his eye, not despite it.**

### Values / avoids
- **Values:** authenticity, dignity of Egyptian popular culture, emotional
  truth, autonomy/final say, place-specificity, sound.
- **Avoids:** exoticism/orientalism, sterile over-production, trend-chasing,
  gloss without meaning, being directed by committee.

### Adjectives
**Visceral · cinematic · vernacular · defiant · textured · intimate**

## COMPARISON

### Shared DNA to build on
1. **The same sun.** Both build images from hard Egyptian/Gulf light on
   mineral architecture — Behiry's souk shadow-play and Mardini's dusk Nile
   frames are cousins, not strangers.
2. **The same palette.** Terracotta, dust, cream, whitewash, oxide red. Put
   their location work side by side and it's one world, graded two ways.
3. **The frontal tableau.** Behiry's centered figure-against-architecture and
   Mardini's microbus brass-band tableau are the same compositional instinct —
   symmetry, frontality, figure-to-facade. This is the strongest formal bridge.
4. **Both literally shot Nubia.** Behiry as a Marie Claire fashion backdrop;
   Mardini as documentary subject ("To The Kids of Nubia"). Same location,
   opposite ethics — the overlap AND the clash in one anecdote.
5. Both are fluent in regional celebrity and fashion codes; Mardini's GQ/Vogue
   work proves he can deliver styled fashion when he wants to.

### Where they'll clash on set — bluntly
- **Control vs. instinct.** Behiry pre-visualizes, pre-lights, shot-lists;
  Mardini chases what the day gives him. Behiry's schedule dies the moment
  Mardini "hears something"; Mardini's spontaneity dies waiting for a re-light.
- **Finish vs. texture.** Behiry's deliverable is retouched, grainless,
  flawless. Mardini's grade is grainy, muted, imperfect-on-purpose. One shared
  grade will feel "dirty" to Behiry's clients and "plastic" to Mardini.
- **People.** Behiry empties the frame of locals; Mardini fills it with them.
  Behiry poses models; Mardini street-casts humans. Asked to shoot Behiry's
  way, Mardini will privately (or loudly) call it orientalist staging — he has
  said versions of this to the press. Asked to shoot Mardini's way, Behiry will
  see uncontrollable variables threatening the garment.
- **Authorship.** This is the big one. Mardini is a self-declared dominant
  director who built his own production company to avoid taking notes. Behiry's
  entire value **is** control of the frame. Two people cannot own the same
  frame. Neither has publicly co-created at this level before — there is no
  evidence either knows how to share a set.
- **Tempo & politics.** Mardini works in emotional/cultural stakes; Behiry in
  deliverables and client sign-off. When the client's feedback arrives, Behiry
  will accommodate it and Mardini will fight it — in front of the crew if
  you let it happen there.

### Non-negotiables (don't ask them to abandon these)
- **El Behiry:** garment legibility and immaculate condition; a controlled key
  on the hero subject; his retouch/finish standard on hero assets; color
  discipline per scene.
- **Mardini:** real people in real places; no exoticizing or costume-ing
  locals; natural-light bias and texture in the grade; sound treated as
  content; a meaningful authorial stake — he will not operate as a hired arm.

### Weak assumptions — flagged honestly
- Behiry's dossier is built almost entirely **from his own website**; there is
  no press record, so his temperament, process, and on-set behavior are
  inferred from the images, not documented. The read "controlled and
  client-safe" is high-confidence from the work; "no ego" is not established.
- "Vogue" and "Marie Claire" on his site are **unverified as to edition**
  (almost certainly Arabia/regional). Tier claims should not be inflated in
  the treatment.
- Mardini's public persona is partly **self-narrated mythmaking** amplified by
  friendly press (CairoScene); the "200-person shoot" is his own claim. He is
  23 with a two-to-three-year professional record — his consistency across a
  long, client-noted campaign is unproven.
- Instagram for both was login-walled; follower/engagement claims are
  secondhand.

## MEET-IN-THE-MIDDLE CREATIVE DIRECTION

### Unifying thesis (one line both can sign)
**"We stage nothing but the frame: real places, real people, one immaculately
lit subject — couture-grade control inside a living Egypt."**

Behiry hears: the hero, the garment, and the light stay mine — precise,
graphic, disciplined. Mardini hears: the world stays real — no emptied streets,
no costumed locals, no orientalist set-dressing. Both are true simultaneously.

### Shared visual language — concrete enough to shoot from
- **Light:** One hard single source per scene — real sun where possible, one
  HMI mimicking sun where not. Behiry's graphic shadow geometry is the look;
  Mardini's rule that it must read as natural is the constraint. Golden-hour
  and dusk windows own the schedule. Controlled bounce/negative fill on the
  hero only; the surrounding world takes the light as it falls. No lit
  backgrounds, no gels.
- **Palette & grade:** Mineral Egyptian palette — terracotta, dust, cream,
  whitewash, Nile blue-grey — with **one saturated accent per scene**
  (Behiry's color discipline). Grade: warm highlights, gently lifted blacks,
  fine 35mm-style grain and mild halation (Mardini's texture). Retouch is
  scoped in writing: garment cleanup and continuity fixes yes; skin
  plasticity, crowd removal, and location "cleaning" no.
- **Camera behavior:** Two registers, explicitly owned. (1) **Hero tableaux**
  — locked, frontal, symmetrical, figure-to-facade, garment fully legible:
  Behiry's frames, but inhabited like Mardini's microbus image, never emptied.
  (2) **Connective tissue** — handheld FX3 movement between tableaux, embedded
  in the real rhythm of the location; slow motion reserved for movement/dance
  beats only. The film breathes handheld and lands on locked frames; the
  stills campaign is the locked frames.
- **Casting:** One professional hero (model or celebrity) + street-cast real
  ensemble with names, actions, and beats — participants, not props, and never
  wardrobe-styled into costume. Hero styling can be couture-level precise.
- **Wardrobe:** Immaculate garments played against vernacular texture — steam
  and garment-handling protocol on set (Behiry's standard), worn in real
  environments without protective staging (Mardini's standard).
- **Location treatment:** Real, working locations shot inside their own rhythm
  — not cleared, not dressed. Each location gets a written split: a one-hour
  controlled hero window (Behiry pre-lights during Mardini's coverage), and
  open coverage time around it. Mardini leads the scout; Behiry leads the
  pre-light test day.
- **Sound (film deliverables):** location sound recorded seriously and used;
  score added only where diegetic sound can't carry.

### What each contributes that the other can't
- **El Behiry:** the hero frame — garment discipline, luxury-client-safe
  finish, graphic still-image precision, studio craft, and Gulf luxury-market
  fluency. He makes the campaign sellable and the key art immaculate.
- **Mardini:** the spine — narrative, place-as-character, emotional access to
  real communities (moulids, street casts, musicians), motion language, sound,
  and international culture-title credibility. He makes the campaign mean
  something and travel beyond the region.

### Three reference touchstones for the blend
1. **Youssef Nabil** (hand-tinted portraits) — the historical Egyptian
   blueprint for exactly this synthesis: staged, glamorous, meticulously
   finished images rooted in Egyptian popular culture without exoticism.
2. **Jamie Hawkesworth's campaign work (Loewe / JW Anderson)** — proof that a
   global luxury campaign can run on natural light, grain, and street-adjacent
   casting while the garment stays sacred. This is the finish target.
3. **_Atlantics_ (Mati Diop, DP Claire Mathon)** — dusk, available light, and
   non-professional casting graded with couture-level color control; the
   motion-picture reference for the palette and the dignity of the gaze.
   (Alternate if you want a stills/campaign name instead: **Gabriel Moses**.)

### The single biggest risk — and how to defuse it in the treatment
**Authorship collision.** Mardini is, by his own public account, an
instinct-driven director who dominates his sets and built a company to avoid
answering to anyone; Behiry's entire professional value is control of the
frame. If the treatment leaves "who owns the image" ambiguous, you will lose
one of them by day two — most likely Behiry going quiet and compliant while
resenting it, or Mardini steamrolling until the client assets suffer.

**Defusal — write the split into the treatment as shot-level ownership, not
vibes:** Mardini owns motion, sequence, ensemble direction, and sound; Behiry
owns the hero frame — light on talent, garment, stills, and the key-art
finish. The tableau list is co-signed in prep and then each shot has exactly
one owner on the day. Joint sign-off happens twice only: the pre-light test
(where the grade reference is locked with both in the room) and final grade.
Client feedback routes through you alone — never to either artist directly.
Frame the whole document in the language above ("we stage nothing but the
frame") so each reads his own non-negotiable as the project's premise rather
than a concession.
